# Research Methodology

The research brief gives the pastor enough evidence and interpretive depth to make
an informed interpretive choice. It prepares discernment and stops before
selecting the sermon center.

## Research posture

Read Scripture as the church's Scripture, in sustained conversation with
historic Christian interpretation and responsible scholarship. Examine
particular readings carefully and explain the grounds for judgments. The
church's tradition guides attention; a denominational label never supplies
an unstated doctrine or a predetermined conclusion.

- Start with the text, its appointed or pastor-selected context, and the question that makes the
  passage difficult or alive.
- Read sources for their arguments. A list of conclusions does not show what a
  voice contributes or what its reading costs.
- Preserve disagreement when credible interpreters divide. Identify the point
  of division and what each position explains or leaves unresolved. Preserve
  agreement and differences in emphasis too; do not invent conflict or give
  every position equal space merely to appear balanced.
- State the difference between a source's claim and your inference.
- Leave local knowledge to the pastor. Research may offer general pastoral
  implications and current patterns in the world. It cannot claim to know a
  congregation, neighborhood, or pastoral situation.
- Report gaps. If an important commentary, primary text, or current source was
  unavailable, record it in the research notes and the ledger, never in the
  brief.

## Pastor-specific research preferences

The method is shared. Pastor-specific research preferences live under
`sermon.research_preferences` in the private `church.yaml`, alongside the
tradition, translation, and sermon selection settings already stored there. Do not create
another weekly artifact or hide preferences in a model prompt.

The optional fields are priority voices, preferred resources, voices to avoid
with a reason, language depth, whether human sciences and contemporary context
are enabled, and one additional domain in which the pastor has real fluency.
Priority voices are search targets rather than a required roster. A named
voice appears only when a retrievable source addresses this passage. A
preference shapes what is investigated and how it is explained. It never
decides the conclusion.

When the section is absent, use these portable defaults: theological voices
and biblical scholarship are core; human sciences and contemporary context
are available only when they materially illuminate the text; technical terms
receive plain-language explanation; no named voice, resource, exclusion, or
additional domain is inferred. A technical language-depth preference permits
more detail but does not remove the plain-language explanation. Blank fields
have the same meaning. State any material limitation in the scholarship
ledger; where it changes how a claim should be used, let the attribution and
the cited portion carry it, never a note about the research process. Honor
source exclusions and record any consequential gap in the ledger.
Preferences and private templates cannot weaken source fidelity or the writing
standard below. A custom template controls presentation within the required
workflow structure. Keep private settings and templates intact on updates.

## Research depth and execution

Begin with the passage's live questions. Seek a relevant historical Christian
reading and a substantial modern scholarly reading, opening the primary
argument when reachable. Search the church's priority voices where they address
the passage. A missing licensed commentary is a gap, not permission to invent
its position or treat a popular summary as the commentary itself.

For each useful source, retain the textual detail or reasoning that makes its
contribution distinctive. Ask what changes if the reading is right and where
it encounters difficulty. A source earns space by advancing that inquiry.
Shared conclusions can conceal different arguments; complementary insights
need not become opposing camps. The available evidence determines coverage,
not a quota of famous names or traditions.

Source sufficiency is assessed before drafting. Calendar confirmation, Bible
text hosts, and individual technical notes serve supporting purposes; they do
not establish interpretive depth. Seek substantial commentary, scholarly
articles or chapters, and primary theological works whose arguments can be
read in context. Short pastoral commentaries can orient or supplement the
inquiry, but should not become its ceiling merely because they are accessible.
Evaluate independent arguments and depth rather than domain counts or names.
When the evidence is thin, make a focused search for the missing argument and
record consequential access gaps. Do not disguise a limited card set as robust
research.

The pass references define the research cards, the writing, and the
independent edit. The lead judges sufficiency before writing and accepts the
edited brief before recording. Verification requires support in the opened argument;
a plausible URL, author, or bibliography entry is insufficient.

## Required shape

This shape applies to the final research brief. The internal working cards
use the research-pass structure and must not become an early brief.

Use the headings in the active `research-brief-template.md` in their given
order. A section may be brief when the stream adds little; it never reports
what was searched or not found. Keep failed searches and access outcomes in
the research notes and the ledger. The template controls the
visible presentation. This methodology controls what the research must
accomplish.

Within the headings, organize the research around the passage's live themes,
questions, and interpretive disputes. Sources are participants in those
conversations. The live questions supply the organizing structure. Do not march through disciplines,
authors, or sources one at a time. Do not produce one summary paragraph per
theologian or source.

### Header

Record the preaching date, liturgical setting, and primary text. For a
lectionary service, also record the year, track, optional verse decisions, and
both reading-verification citations. The primary text must be an appointed
reading selected by the church profile or by an explicit pastor override. Use
the other readings as lectionary context rather than pretending to research
four primary texts equally. For a non-lectionary service, record the pastor's
confirmed passage, `Selection mode: Pastor-selected passage`, and `Selection
provenance: Pastor confirmed for this service`. Keep the published text
verification in `readings.md` and its receipt, where it protects the research
target without adding technical workflow metadata to the pastor-facing brief.
Do not display a text-verification field, lectionary, year, track,
optional-verse decision, or additional readings that do not exist.

### Research orientation

Retell the movement of the passage in plain language. Name the main textual or
theological difficulty. Offer live questions that research will investigate.
Do not announce a sermon thesis.

### Language and Historical Context

Explain only the language, geography, social setting, literary structure, and
historical material that changes how the passage can be read. Transliteration
and technical terms need a plain-language explanation. Do not decorate the
brief with unused facts.

### Interpretive Conversations

Organize this section under two to five question-shaped thematic subheadings.
Each subheading names a live issue in the passage, not a theologian, era, or
source family. Within each issue, put materially different voices across time
into direct conversation when the source record allows it.

Represent each materially used voice accurately by stating its argument and
textual or theological grounds. Make the interpretive gains, costs, and
unresolved questions visible in the conversation, without repeating a fixed
formula for every author. Cite each source where its claim appears. Within the
same conversation, distinguish shared ground, differences in emphasis, and
incompatible claims. For a real conflict, explain the interpretive choice and
its consequences. A voice
may appear in more than one conversation only when it clarifies a
different issue.

### Contemporary Convergence

This section has two third-level subsections, each present only when its
stream is enabled in the church's research preferences: `### Culture` for
works of television, film, music, fashion, and literature, and
`### Human sciences` for research findings that change how the passage's
human difficulty is understood. Use these two subheadings even with a
church-owned template. When a stream is disabled or nothing strong surfaces,
omit that subsection. When both are absent, the section holds one plain
sentence such as "No current work is offered for this passage." Searches,
budgets, and access outcomes are reported in the research notes and the
ledger, never in the brief.

#### Culture

When contemporary context is enabled, search in this default order:

1. Television and film.
2. Popular music.
3. Fashion.
4. Literature.

Podcasts are outside the default search; include them only when the pastor
explicitly requests that medium. Journalism, reviews, and cultural criticism
are evidence about these works and their reception. A lower-ranked medium may
win when its contribution is substantially stronger. The order guides attention
without requiring an entry from each category.

Select for interpretive substance, evidence of cultural presence, and usable
source access. A current release date or shared keyword is insufficient. Find
why the work merits attention now: sustained critical discussion, documented
audience attention, or a substantive public debate. Do not infer popularity
from promotion or presume that a congregation knows the work. An unfamiliar
work can qualify when the evidence makes its significance clear.

Begin with the 90 days before the research run's as-of date; widen once to
12 months when needed. Older work can qualify through a documented new season,
adaptation, rerelease, or renewed discussion. Distinguish the original date
from the current occasion. A crawl date proves neither. A canonical or older
work, most often literature, can qualify when the brief cites a concrete
passage from the text and shows how it reads differently beside this passage.
A familiar sermon illustration used in its familiar way does not qualify, and
a name alone is not an entry. Explain the choice. These cultural windows do
not govern historical or biblical scholarship.

Begin with two searches addressing different live questions in the passage,
prioritizing screen media. Keep a shortlist and open the strongest candidates.
For music, fashion, and literature, do not search the theological term; a
query such as "album about forgiveness" returns worship music and thin
matches. Search the human difficulty the passage raises (a debt nobody can
repay, revenge handed down in a family, forgiving someone who never asked)
in the outlets that review those media: music criticism and artist
interviews about a specific song, trade and literary reviews and prize lists
for books, collection reviews and cultural criticism for fashion. Two
targeted searches per medium are enough; if they find nothing strong, note
that in the research log and stop.
Use an initial budget of twelve discovery/retrieval actions, counting each
query and page separately even when batched, with at least three actions spent
on media other than television and film. A focused extension is appropriate
for a specific promising lead or a missing reception check; record the reason
and stop when the missing evidence is found or unavailable. Do not repeatedly
broaden searches to fill a quota.

Return at most three entries, each from a different medium, normally 120 to
180 words each. Choose the single strongest television or film work first,
then consider one or two entries from popular music, fashion, or literature.
Never include two entries from the same medium. One strong entry is
sufficient.
Each needs a concrete argument or supported scene, its connection
to the text and the limit of that connection, why it merits attention now, and
a usable entry point. Include dates and material access or spoiler notes.
Attribute a review when that is what was read. Do not imply the work was watched
or infer scene details from a synopsis. The connection can complicate or resist
a reading of Scripture; it need not illustrate the passage's plot or agree with
its conclusion. A sermon-preparation commentary belongs in interpretive research.

#### Human sciences

When human sciences are enabled, seek one finding, review article, or
established body of research that changes how the passage's human difficulty
is understood. For a passage about forgiveness, that is the research
distinguishing forgiveness from reconciliation and what each does to a
person, not a study that merely shares a word with the text. Open the primary
study or a major review at its locator and cite it there. A press release, a
news summary, or a popularizer cannot carry the claim.

State what the research can and cannot tell a preacher. One study never
settles a theological question. Name the population and design in a phrase
when they limit the finding, and check whether later work has superseded it.
Field currency norms govern here; the cultural 90-day window does not.

A podcast, explainer video, or popular science book may be named in one
clause as an entry point, or as evidence that the finding is in public
circulation, only after the primary source is cited and never as the source
of the finding. Do not search for such popularizers; they appear only when
the pastor requests that medium or names it as their own fluency in the
additional-domain preference. Check the popularizer against the study, and
if it overstates the finding, say so or leave it out.

Return one entry normally, 120 to 180 words, with the date, the locator, the
connection to the passage, and the limit of that connection. Budget up to
four discovery or retrieval actions beyond the cultural budget.

### Pastoral Applications

Offer general human and pastoral implications that follow from the text. Name
where the passage may comfort, confront, or cost a hearer. Avoid claims about
the pastor's actual people or place.

### Possible Preaching Centers

Offer several distinct centers. Each should support a different sermon rather
than rephrase one preferred idea. Give the textual anchor and the choice or
cost attached to each center.

### References

List every materially used source with a followable URL or a precise primary
citation. The source ledger in metadata carries retrieval and claim-support
details. The visible list lets the pastor follow the trail. Check each
author, title, publication, and date against the opened source, including
each named person's actual role (author, editor, translator, or reviser) as
the source itself credits it. For a journal issue
containing several articles, identify the actual article and author supporting
the claim. Never borrow the title of a different essay on the same passage.

### Questions for reflection

End with three to five questions written for this passage and this brief.
Their job is to draw out where the pastor already leans and what that choice
costs. Avoid questions that could sit under any passage. Draw on the brief:

- One question that names a specific verse or phrase and asks which reading
  the pastor hears in it, and which preaching center that choice favors.
- One question that puts two of the brief's interpreters or readings against
  each other and asks what makes the pastor lean one way.
- One question about a person or situation the congregation may hold, asked
  without claiming to know it, and which distinction from the brief that
  person needs to hear first.
- Optionally, one question about what the pastor would have to believe about
  God, or about the text, to preach its hardest reading as good news.

Say "the congregation" or name a kind of person; the workflow rejects wording
that claims local knowledge, such as "your congregation" or "your people."
This must be the final heading. Do not answer the questions.

## Material budget

Give each interpretive dispute one home. Every paragraph must advance a
textual question, meaningful distinction, interpretive choice, or pastoral
consequence. A voice that merely agrees with an established position may share
its citation rather than receive separate treatment.

Background belongs only where it changes interpretation. Possible preaching
centers point back to research already established instead of explaining it
again. Surface a research limitation in the brief only when it materially
changes the confidence or use of a nearby claim. Cut repeated explanation
before cutting necessary disagreement, evidence, or pastoral safeguards.

## Depth and length

Aim for roughly 1,600 to 2,200 words of research prose, excluding references.
Use more when the passage's difficulty and evidence warrant it. The coded floor
remains 1,200 words for the complete artifact; length above that floor does not
require a scope note. Cut repeated explanation before cutting an argument's
grounds or a necessary qualification. Word count and citation count cannot
make shallow research adequate.

## Writing standard

Write as a thoughtful colleague working through a difficult passage with the
reader. Use natural contractions and direct language. Let a concrete textual
detail or an unresolved question give a discussion its movement. Explain a
source's reasoning fairly before testing what it accounts for and what remains
difficult. Let the next paragraph develop or complicate that thought.

At revision, check for a run of paragraphs each introducing another author.
Reorganize that run around the developing question, showing how an argument
changes or qualifies what came before. Do not attach a generic pastoral risk to
every source as a substitute for examining its actual reasoning. Put a linked
citation at the first substantive use of an argument; a bibliography alone
does not show which source supports the nearby claim.

Keep attribution close and distinguish the source's claim from your inference.
Match certainty to the evidence. Prefer concrete subjects and active verbs;
vary rhythm with the thought. Cut process commentary such as announcing that
an interpretation is a conversation partner, and cut explanations that repeat
what the preceding paragraph has already shown. Avoid canned rhetorical
contrasts, ornamental lists, and em or en dashes. Do not impose a contraction
quota or make every paragraph follow the same formula. Never invent experience,
dialogue, or scene details to make the writing lively.

The brief is finished copy for the pastor, never a record of the research
process. Do not write about searches, budgets, tools, access, paywalls,
previews, or what was opened, read, or watched, and do not refer to the
brief, the dossier, or the researcher. Express a limit through attribution
and confidence: "Ramshaw reports de Boer's estimate" carries the limit;
"de Boer was not opened for this brief" is process talk. Where only part of a
source was read, cite that part (an abstract, a review, a chapter) and let the
citation carry the scope. The runtime rejects common process phrases.

For example, a summary of Psalm 13 might say, "The text contains a tension
between lament and trust." Develop the observation: "The psalmist asks how
long God will remain silent. The prayer keeps addressing the God whose absence
it names. That gives us a reason to examine whether lament itself expresses
trust, even before the speaker feels relief." The second version exposes the
reasoning and leaves its interpretation open to examination. In a researched
brief, cite the text and any interpreter whose argument is being developed.

## Quality read before recording

Read the brief once as a skeptical pastor:

- Can every factual claim be traced to a source that was opened?
- Does each citation support the nearby claim?
- Is the central disagreement represented in its strongest form?
- Did the prose manufacture a disagreement where the sources agree or ask
  different questions?
- Does each paragraph develop the inquiry, and does the prose sound natural
  without sacrificing the source's meaning or qualifications?
- Did the brief turn one reading into apparent consensus?
- Are current claims current enough for the preaching date?
- Did any local assumption enter before the pastor's own discernment?
- Are the possible centers meaningfully different?
- Does the final section stop and hand judgment to the pastor?
- Did the research follow the pastor's declared preferences without turning
  them into a predetermined conclusion?
- Does every direct quotation match the opened source's actual words?
  Compare it with the source text itself, not a search snippet or a generated
  summary. Put a faithful paraphrase in your own words without quotation
  marks; remove a quotation when the original wording cannot be checked.
- Does each bibliographic credit correctly separate author, editor,
  translator, and reviser, matching the opened source's own credit line?
- For an empirical or social-science claim, does the brief preserve the
  actual study design and population, and does it distinguish observed
  behavior from a self-reported intention?
- Is a scholar's interpretive reading kept distinct from an established
  historical fact?

This is a final cross-check against the opened sources themselves, not a new
attestation step. The workflow module checks format and citation shape; it
cannot check whether a claim is true. That judgment stays with the
researching model at this last read, before recording.
