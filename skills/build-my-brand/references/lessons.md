# Lessons: the teach-and-choose rounds

Each round is one lesson, two or three options (or, in refinement, one
tightened system), and one decision. Give the lesson in a few short
paragraphs, in church language, then show the work, then ask. Write the
lesson into the guide section so the pastor can reread it. The interface
opens every section with where the pastor is and what comes next, and
appends the options, what was shown, the decision, and why.

The lessons below are the substance to teach, not a script to paste. Adapt
them to what discovery found and what the brief says.

## Round 1: `identity`. One identity, many uses

A church is one institution to the people it serves, even when it holds
several ministries, a school, a food pantry, or a choir. When each program
makes its own logo, the church stops looking like one place. That is the most
common way a small church brand falls apart, and it happens one well-meant
flyer at a time.

The alternative is a design language the whole church shares. Programs get
lockups (the parent mark with the program's name held in a fixed position)
or bespoke images made from the system's own parts, so a new weeknight
program reads as a sibling of the church and not a second brand competing
with it. Either way, a volunteer or an agent can name a new program without
designing anything.

The choice: confirm that programs, events, and ministries live inside one
identity, or name a real exception and why it earns its own mark. The
brief's `name` answer already says which institutions the name holds; this
round confirms how they relate visually.

## Round 2: `direction`. One idea before any mark

A brand direction is the single idea everything else expresses. It is not a
logo and not a color; it is the answer to "what is this church, said in one
line, that a stranger could feel from across the street." Without it, the
mark, the palette, and the type get chosen separately and never add up.

Directions are built from what the brief and discovery said, not from
adjectives. Each direction has a name, a one-paragraph story, a palette with
jobs, a type territory, a reference board, and real applications in that
type and color: the bulletin cover, the church's own test announcement, and
the website's first screen, with a plain placeholder where the mark goes.
No mark is shown; on the refresh path the kept mark appears unmodified. Two
or three directions is right; more than three splits attention.

Say plainly what the pick commits and what it leaves open: the idea, and the
territory color and type will explore, not these layouts, colors, or
typefaces. The pastor asked exactly this on the proof run.

The choice: pick the direction. The pastor may borrow one element from
another direction; record that as part of the decision and explain what it
costs in coherence, if anything.

## Round 3: `develop`. A system is developed as a whole

A brand is a system, and the parts of a system change each other. The mark
decides how big the name can be; the type decides what the mark sits
beside; the colors decide whether the mark reverses on the sign; the image
language decides what a Tuesday announcement looks like. A mark designed
alone, in black, before any of this, comes out looking like everyone else's,
however carefully it is drawn. So the direction is developed whole, by the
designer who made its board.

What the pastor sees: two or three developed versions of their direction,
each a real composition on the same applications as the direction boards
and more, now with a mark inside the system: the bulletin cover, the test
announcement, the website screen, the sign at street distance, the social
avatar, and beside a stand-in partner mark where the brief has one. The
versions differ in how the mark and system go, not in color alone. The firm
recommends one and says why. Behind the boards sits wide exploration the
pastor does not have to sit through; the guide shows a small sample of what
was set aside and why, so the pastor knows how many ideas were considered.

The choice: which version to carry forward. The pastor may say what to take
from another version; record it.

## Rounds 4 and 5: `refine-1`, `refine-2`. Tightening, not reopening

A refinement round makes the chosen version more itself. The idea does not
change; the spacing, the weights, the way the name sits, the exact crop,
the way the announcement composes, all get tighter and more real. Two
rounds are enough when the direction and the version were right; more
rounds mean the earlier decision was wrong, and the honest move then is to
say so.

What the pastor sees: the tightened system on the same applications, more
real each time, with the church's actual content where possible. What the
pastor is asked: reactions, not adjectives. What reads, what does not, what
they would be proud to hand someone. The second refinement ends with the
mark the church is keeping, recorded as an editable vector.

## Round 6: `color`. What each color is for

A palette is a small set of colors with jobs, not a set of favorites. One
color leads; it goes on the sign and the mark. One or two support it. Paper
and ink are colors too. Every color has to meet a contrast test so words
stay readable for older eyes and on cheap printers; say what the test is and
show that the palette passes.

Color is confirmed on the developed system, because a color that looks
right on a swatch can fail on the sign or fight the announcement. Print and
screen differ: a color that glows on a phone can go muddy on the bulletin.
Show each color as it prints and as it displays, on the real applications.
Where the tradition has colors of its own, seasonal liturgical colors for
example, the brand palette sits beside them rather than fighting them.

The choice: the final palette, with each color's job named. The five colors
the bulletin renderer reads (`ink`, `accent`, `accent_deep`, `paper`,
`rubric_red`) are set from this palette.

## Round 7: `type`. Type carries most of a brand

Most of what a church prints is words, so the type is most of the brand.
Two families are enough: one for headlines and the name, one for everything
else. They should be open-licensed so any volunteer can use them on any
computer without buying anything, and so the website and the bulletin use
the same faces.

Type was developed inside the system; this round confirms it where the
pastor can read it: the bulletin cover, a paragraph of a sermon note, an
event title, the announcement. Explain what each family is doing. A display
face with personality, a text face that disappears into reading. If the
pastor already uses a face for something else, say what sharing it costs.

The choice: the pairing, with the license named.

## Round 8: `voice`. How the church talks

A brand is also how the church sounds. Voice rules keep the website, the
newsletter, and the social posts sounding like the same people. They are
short: how the church names itself, how it addresses a stranger, what it
never says, how long a sentence runs, whether it uses humor and where.

Take three real samples from discovery (a website line, a post, a bulletin
announcement) and rewrite each two or three ways. The pastor picks the
rewrite that sounds like the church. The rules are derived from the picks,
not invented.

The choice: the voice rules, saved to `brand/staging/voice.md` and later read
by every writing workflow.
