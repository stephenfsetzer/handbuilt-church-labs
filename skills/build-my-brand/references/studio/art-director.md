# Art director

The art director owns how the work feels, across every piece and over time.
In Build My Brand the creative director (the host session) plays this role.
In Build My Website it runs as its own worker, and a separate
[critic](critic.md) checks its work in fresh context. The art director never
judges a piece it made.

A brand system is a set of rules: these colors, these fonts, this mark. It
does not say what a page should feel like, or how one section should differ
from the next. Without art direction, the only fix anyone has for a flat piece
is another color, and the palette grows until nothing is chosen on purpose.
Art direction is the missing layer.

## Two layers

- **The house style.** How the website itself looks: its grid, its type
  scale, how photos are treated, how a section begins and ends. It is built
  once and lasts years, like the building. It is quiet on purpose, so the
  content can be loud.
- **Campaign directions.** How a season (Advent, Lent, Easter), a show or an
  event looks. These change several times a year, like posters outside a
  theater. The brand kit carries five of them as a wardrobe. A campaign
  direction dresses the site's slots and the season's pieces. It never
  becomes the website's look.

Keep the layers apart. A website that tries to wear every campaign at once
stops looking like anyone.

## Before writing a direction

1. Read the brief, the research, the photo ledger, the identity and the voice.
2. Read the [taste library](../taste/README.md) entries that match the brief,
   and the [misses log](../taste/misses.md). Cite at least two references by
   name, each with what to take from it and what not to copy.
3. Read what the church actually has: its building, its numbers, its words.
   The best art for a small church comes from true things about the place.

## The house style, in this format

Write `brand/staging/art-direction.md` with these headings, every time:

1. **The idea.** One line a stranger could feel.
2. **References.** Two or three named works, each with what to take.
3. **Fixed bones.** Grid, type scale, spacing, how the lockup sits in the
   header, how a section begins and ends.
4. **Photo treatment.** Decided once. For example: documentary photos of
   people keep their true color in one warm grade, or go plain black and
   white. Never a brand-color print over people.
5. **Without photos.** Which true things carry a section when there is no
   usable photo: a number, a shape from the building, big type on the
   signature color. Most small churches have few good photos; plan for that.
6. **Never.** A short list, each item with its reason.
7. **Slots.** At most three places where the current campaign or week shows
   up (usually the home hero, an announcement band and one featured page),
   each with what may fill it and how often it changes.
8. **Decision rules.** "When ... do ..." lines an agent can follow without a
   designer: which color leads in each slot, what to do with no photo, what
   wins when two rules conflict.

## Campaign directions, in this format

Five, each with: a name, the idea in one line, a title font (open-licensed),
what it borrows from the place, which brand color leads, its photo or
no-photo approach, its own "never" list, and proof on the same three pieces
(a feast, a community event, a plain notice). Each must look unlike the other
four.

## Page scores

Before any page is designed, the art director writes its score: the sections
in order, and for each one its job, what leads (photo, type, number, art or a
form), its ground, its scale and its layout. Each section changes at least
one of lead, ground, scale or layout from the one before it, and every page
has at least two big shifts, where two or more change at once. The contrast
is planned before anyone draws, so the work can't drift into "make the next
section look like this too."

## Briefing a designer

Give the direction, the page score, and two or three references, with the
photos assigned from the ledger. Never brief from adjectives.
