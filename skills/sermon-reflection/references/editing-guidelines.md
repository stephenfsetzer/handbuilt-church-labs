# Editing the pastor's sermon

These are guidelines for judgment, not gates. The test of a good edit is
simple: the pastor reads it and says, "That's mine."

## Start from their sentences

- Keep the pastor's sentences. Change the order before you change the words.
- Write new sentences only to connect parts, and write them in the pastor's
  register. If they talk to the room ("Think about it."), your connecting
  sentences do too.
- Much of the wording in `sermon.md` should come from the page. The `record`
  result reports the share of the sermon's words that are still the pastor's.
  A light edit of a full draft usually keeps half or more; well under a third
  means you rewrote it.

## Keep their voice

Voice lives in habits that a tidy editor removes. Keep them:

- questions to the congregation
- asides and plain teaching moments ("This word comes from...")
- exclamations and emphasis
- repetition used for rhythm
- humor, informality, and the pastor's own name for things
- stories told the way they tell them

Do not flatten these into polished prose. Do not replace their plain words
with more elegant ones.

## Serve their purpose

The pastor said what they hope people feel and do. Hold every change against
that. If the pastor wants people to leave hopeful, do not soften the hope into
caution. A sermon proclaims; a hopeful ending is part of the job, not an
overpromise to correct.

## Must fix

Fix these, and tell the pastor what you fixed and why:

- **Facts and attributions.** A quotation assigned to the wrong person, a
  wrong date or number, a misremembered story about a public figure. Check
  what you can against opened sources. If a detail cannot be confirmed,
  suggest cutting or hedging it.
- **Scripture and liturgy.** Quotations must match the translation and the
  church's liturgy. Check against `readings.md`, the research brief, or an
  opened source. Never correct from memory.
- **Safety.** When a sermon speaks about suicide or self-harm, follow safe
  messaging practice: leave out any method or means, do not describe suicide
  as a solution, and name a next step, such as telling someone they trust.
  Keep the pastor's honesty; remove only the method.
- **Other people's privacy.** When the sermon tells someone else's story in a
  way the congregation could identify, ask the pastor whether that person is
  comfortable with it. Do not decide for them.

## Everything else is a suggestion

- Suggest cuts; do not make them silently. Cut only what the pastor flagged
  ("I don't know where I'm going with this") or what clearly belongs to a
  different sermon, and say so.
- When the sermon drifts from the text, suggest a way back using the research
  brief. Use the pastor's own images where you can.
- Suggest a change in order when it helps the sermon build to the pastor's
  purpose.
- Length: note if the sermon will run much longer or shorter than the pastor's
  usual time. Do not cut to a target on your own.

## What not to do

- Do not add a second story, a quotation, or an illustration the pastor did
  not bring.
- Do not add theological qualifications the pastor did not make, unless a
  claim is wrong.
- Do not balance every strong statement with a caveat.
- Do not turn the sermon into an essay with headings.
- Do not invent a memory, detail, or local fact.

## Telling the pastor what you did

Keep it short:

1. What was already working, in their words.
2. What you had to fix, and why.
3. What you changed as a suggestion they can undo.
4. A few lines to read aloud and check, especially any sentence you wrote.
